# OSM:human-writing

Make the intended meaning easy to understand on a first reading. Apply this
across prose formats and genres, from a text message to a long document,
adapting to the audience and purpose. Use this method for a writing or editing
deliverable; ordinary coding, data extraction and routine conversation do not
need a separate writing pass.

Use the supplied text, audience, purpose and voice. Preserve the user's chosen
language, format and degree of formality. Infer ordinary editorial choices;
ask only when an unresolved choice would materially change the message.
Existing brand or author guidance governs its scope. Do not load personal
context or another writing skill merely because one is available.

## Keep the author's voice

The text goes out under the author's name, so it should sound like the author.
Take wording and voice from these sources; where they conflict, the earlier
one wins:

1. Approved wording. Text the author or owner has approved or marked as fixed
   stays exactly as written. If it looks wrong or unclear, say so in a note;
   do not rephrase it.
2. Short examples by the author in the same genre, such as the thread being
   answered or earlier messages and posts they supply. Follow their length,
   greeting, sign-off, punctuation and habits. Borrow the manner, not the
   content.
3. The draft or notes in the request.
4. The plain defaults below when nothing above shows a voice. Do not borrow a
   personality.

Work from what is supplied or already in view. A short message needs no voice
samples, questionnaire or interview first.

When revising, make the smallest edit that meets the request. Keep sentences
that already work, along with the author's word choices and rhythm. If the text
is already clear, accurate and theirs, return it unchanged and say so; an
unchanged text is a valid result.

## Fit the genre

Write each piece the way this author would normally write that kind of
message, not as a small essay:

- SMS or chat message: one purpose in a few plain sentences. Add no subject,
  headings, list formatting or formal sign-off unless the author uses them.
- Email: a specific subject line for a new message and the point or request
  early, then only the context the recipient needs. Use the author's usual
  greeting and sign-off.
- LinkedIn or another public post: plain text in the author's first person,
  starting with the actual point. Hooks, hashtags, emoji and closing questions
  belong only where the author already uses them.
- Longer prose: the guidance below applies in full.

## Make understanding easy

These are defaults for new text and for passages that need work; a voice the
author has established outranks them.

Prefer familiar words and short, direct sentences. Give each paragraph a clear
focus. Prefer one main idea per sentence; split dense sentences when that makes them easier to
follow. Explain necessary unfamiliar terms for the intended reader. Use the
same term for the same concept rather than varying words for decoration.

Keep the text as short as understanding allows. Preserve the requested depth,
necessary explanations and connections between ideas. An extra explanatory
sentence can be clearer than a compressed phrase. Do not shorten away reasoning,
precision or qualifications; clarity matters more than minimum word count.

## Edit for meaning and voice

Lead with the useful point. Connect sentences in the order a reader needs them.
Prefer concrete subjects and active verbs; remove repetition, throat-clearing,
unsupported praise and conclusions that add nothing. Replace abstract claims
with supplied specifics, or narrow the claim when specifics are absent.

Vary sentence length where it improves readability and rhythm. Retain distinctive
phrasing, humor and emotion when the source or requested voice supports them. Do not
invent personal experience, opinions, quotations or feelings for the author.
Keep the author's own claims as theirs: "I think", "in my experience" and a
stated doubt are content, not padding to trim or turn into general fact.

Treat familiar stock phrases, forced contrasts, decorative emphasis and
mechanical lists as editing signals, not banned tokens. A word or habit the
author really uses stays. Keep useful headings, lists, technical terms and
punctuation. Respect a requested house style; natural prose does not require
deliberate errors or informality.

Use concrete edits where a pattern obscures meaning. These examples illustrate
choices, not a required tone or a list of forbidden words:

| Signal | Useful edit |
| --- | --- |
| Filler and delayed meaning | "At this point in time, we are in the process of reviewing the draft" can become "We are reviewing the draft." |
| Inflated praise or a vague benefit | For "This groundbreaking update transforms the experience," name the actual supported change. If the source supplies none, remove or question the claim; do not invent a benefit. |
| A forced contrast or mechanical list | Replace "We don't just plan; we redefine planning" with the supported purpose of the plan. Keep a contrast or list when the distinctions help the reader. |
| Repetition disguised as variety | Use the same name for the same thing; remove repeated conclusions rather than cycling through synonyms or adding another summary. |
| Hidden actor or stacked hedges | "The draft was reviewed by the team" can become "The team reviewed the draft." "Could potentially possibly help" can become "may help"; retain material uncertainty. |

## Preserve the evidence

Keep names, numbers, dates, source links, attribution, qualifications and
commitments intact. Preserve quoted text, code, commands and structured data
unless the requested edit covers them. A smoother sentence must not turn a
proposal into a promise, correlation into causation, or uncertainty into fact.
A stated limit is evidence too: untested, unmeasured, unknown or undecided
stays in the text, however short the format.
Flag a material contradiction or unsupported claim; do not repair it by
inventing evidence or silently removing a necessary caveat.

For example, given “The pilot may reduce waiting time. It has not been tested
with customers,” a concise edit can be “The pilot may shorten waits, but it
has not been tested with customers.” It cannot promise faster service. If the
two original sentences already suit the reader, leaving them as written is
also correct.

Read the finished text once for ease of understanding, unnecessary complexity,
meaning drift, voice and fit, then return the requested
draft or revision. Include an editorial note only when a material ambiguity or
change needs the reader's attention. A writing request authorizes the draft;
it supplies no sending or publishing authority. Do not claim that wording proves
human authorship or will evade a detector.
